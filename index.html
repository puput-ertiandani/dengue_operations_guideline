Chapter 1. Epidemiology

1.1 Virus Transmission

Key points
  • Spread by bites of infected female Aedes aegypti and albopictus; four serotypes (DENV-1 to 4).
  • Range: no symptoms, DF, DHF, DSS (can be fatal); severe cases need hospital care.
  • Common signs: high fever, headache, body aches, nausea, rash. In pregnancy: pre-term birth, low birthweight, fetal distress.

Dengue is a mosquito borne viral disease caused by the dengue virus (DENV), which transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitos.

By now, dengue has four serotypes infect human bodies namely DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4. Each serotypes cause different symptoms and severity which clinical spectrum from asymptomatic infected or with mild fever to moderate dengue fever (DF), dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS) that can cause fatal result to death. The most common symptoms are high fever, headache, body aches, nausea and rash. But not all of infected people show symptoms. Infected people with mild effect can get better in 1-2 weeks with no special treatment. However, patients with severe cases should be hospitalised to recover. To pregnant women, dengue can cause pre-term birth, low birthweight and fetal distress to the babies. [1-4]

1.2 Case Rate

Key points
  • 100-400 million infections a year; endemic in 100+ countries, Asia at highest risk.
  • Cases reported to WHO: 505,430 (2000) to 14.6 million (2024).
  • Drivers: climate change, urbanisation, mobility, socioeconomic status, knowledge. Vaccines still under evaluation.

According to WHO, 100-400 million incidences occurred annually around the world, that the dengue is currently endemic in more than 100 countries with many Asian countries are listed in the highest risk rank.[3, 4]

The dengue cases has grown dramatically globally in recent decades, with the number of cases reported to WHO increasing from 505,430 cases in 2000 to 14.6 million in 2024. Dengue disease remains a recurring and escalating public health risk across the region, driven by climate change, urbanisation, and population mobility that also associate with socioeconomic status and population knowledge. This disease is commonly found in tropical and sub-tropical climate worldwide, that make dengue also classified as one of 17 Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTD). Moreover, dengue is mostly found in urban and semi-urban population settings. Dengue vaccines have been available in some countries, yet the effectiveness is still under evaluation.[2, 4, 6-8]

1.3 Environmental and Social Factors

Key points
  • Spread depends on density, mobility, water access and storage, and community knowledge, attitudes and practices.

Dengue transmission may occur through multiple social and environmental factors: population density, human mobility, access to reliable water source, water storage practices, etc. Therefore, whether the spread or prevention of diseases within population depends on community knowledge, attitudes and practices towards dengue.[3]

1.3.1 Environmental and Climatic Conditions

Key points
  • Best for mosquitoes: 18-35°C, 60-80% humidity, 200-800 mm rain; risk rises at rain onset for 3+ months.
  • Cases follow rainfall by about two months (2.3.1 says 2-4 weeks: check).
  • Risk is spreading to suburban and rural areas; floods add water-borne disease.

Climate change leads to increasing temperatures, high rainfall and humidity, giving a perfect condition for mosquitoes breeding site, which increases the risk of mosquitoes borne diseases transmission like dengue. With temperatures ranging from 18° C - 35° C, humidity ranging from 60% - 80%, and rainfall in the range of 200-800 mm are suitable for mosquito life and transmission of the dengue virus to humans. Increased dengue fever risk is at the onset of rainfall, lasting for at least three months.[2, 4, 5]

When the rainy season begins, the mosquito eggs that hibernate during the dry season will soon hatch. In relation to this hatching and the growth to adulthood, the pattern of dengue cases generally followed the rainfall around of two months. However, the interaction between the dengue virus, the host and the environment is dynamic. Vectors can adapt to new environments and climate. Consequently, dengue risks may change and shift with climate change in combination with increased urbanisation and population movement. It may cause the increasing of dengue cases also occur in suburban or rural areas, either lower or higher land area which are not in the same density as urban areas.[2, 4]

The risk becomes worse when environment and climatic changes also lead to hazardous event, such as extreme weather, heavy rainfall, and flooding. Not only dengue, another diseases (eg. Water borne diseases) may follow.

1.3.2 Community Awareness

Key points
  • Low awareness leads to uncovered water, poor waste and discarded tyres, which become breeding sites.
  • Higher risk: informal settlements, low income, elderly, pregnant and lactating women, children, persons with disabilities, chronic illness.

Lack of awareness contribute to inadequate preventive behaviours, then lead to virus transmission. Low community awareness is associated with lack of knowledge about dengue. Communities may not be able to diagnose early symptoms of dengue. Limited awareness influence the community behaviour that increases the risk of dengue transmission, as follows: poor waste disposal, leaving uncovered water storage containers, discarded tyres, plastic containers and other water-holding materials provide ideal breeding habitats for Aedes mosquitoes. These challenges are common in dense populations with inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene services. Moreover, the risk becomes higher among the vulnerable people, such as: who are living in informal settlements, low-income communities, elderly, pregnant and lactating women, children, persons with disabilities and people living with chronic diseases.

1.3.3 Population Mobility and Cross-Border Transmission

Key points
  • Cross-border movement spreads dengue and complicates surveillance; coordinate preparedness across borders.

Population mobility and cross-border movement contribute to the continued spread of dengue across area even countries—as some countries in Asia Pacific are lied in the same island with high cross-border mobility. This mobility complicates surveillance, early detection and outbreak control and increases the need for coordinated preparedness and anticipatory action.

Chapter 2. Prevention, Preparedness and Early Action

Prevention aims to reduce the impact of hazards and vulnerability[9] by trying to stop emergencies before they happen. By eliminating or reducing high-risk factors, organisations and communities can significantly minimise the impact of future crises. The prevention phase focuses on identifying hazards early and taking proactive steps to eliminate or reduce the likelihood of an incident occurring. This stage is centred on risk mitigation and long-term safety improvements.[10]

According to United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), preparedness is the knowledge and capacities developed by governments, response and recovery organisations, communities and individuals to effectively anticipate, respond to and recover from the impacts of likely, imminent or current disasters. Preparedness includes such activities as contingency planning, the stockpiling of equipment and supplies, the development of arrangements for coordination, public information, and field exercises. A preparedness plan is needed to enable timely, effective and appropriate responses to specific potential hazardous events or emerging disaster situations that might threaten society or the environment.[11]

Early warning and early action, also called as anticipatory action or forecast-based action. It means efforts to protect people before a disaster strikes including outbreak based on early warning or forecasts. It needs meaningful engagement with at-risk communities to ensure its effectiveness.[12] Figure 1 below is a schematic map from disease outbreak preparedness to recovery phase.[13] It shows that well anticipatory action or early action could reduce significantly the impact of outbreak trajectory compared to conventional humanitarian response.

2.1 Dengue Prevention

Key points
  • Control = surveillance + integrated vector management; community behaviour is central.

Dengue is mostly found in tropical and sub-tropical climates worldwide, mostly in urban and semi-urban areas. However recent cases show the increased prevalence in rural areas across some countries in Asia Pacific. Control relies mainly on surveillance and integrated vector interventions. Surveillance of infectious disease is defined as the systematic reporting, monitoring and data analysis of cases. Community behaviours really play an important role to prevent the dengue outbreak, including water storage, plant-keeping and self protection against mosquito bites. Routine vector surveillance and control activities among community could improve resilience.[2-4, 6]

2.1.1 Promoting Personal Protection

Key points
  • Cover the body, use nets, screens, repellents, coils and vaporisers; promote LLINs; adapt to local supply and authority guidance.

Preventive action could start from each community members to protect themselves first. There are some preventive actions as personal responsibility as suggested by World Health Organisation (WHO)[3] as following:

  • Wearing clothes that cover as much of body as possible
  • Using mosquito nets, ideally sprayed with insect repellent
  • Using window screens or closing window
  • Applying mosquito repellents
  • Using mosquito-control supplies, including mosquito coils and vaporisers
  • The distribution and promotion of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs)

The kinds of mentioned items could be contextualised based on local availability and public authorities guidance.

2.1.2 Health and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) Promotion

Key points
  • Household and community actions reduce breeding sites; door-to-door and school activities work well.
  • Deliver: health and WASH campaigns (safe water storage), breeding-site reduction with larvicide guidance, behaviour change through regular engagement.

Health and WASH promotion are important components of dengue prevention because many actions to reduce dengue transmission take place at the household and community level. Community members can contribute to reducing mosquito breeding opportunities through appropriate water storage, management of containers and other potential breeding sites, and regular maintenance of the household and surrounding environment.

Experiences from National Societies indicate that health and hygiene promotion, elimination of standing water, and destruction of mosquito breeding sites are commonly implemented as part of dengue prevention and vector control activities. Door-to-door visits and school-based activities have also been identified as effective approaches for raising awareness and encouraging preventive behaviours at household and community levels.

There are some good practices the National Society could adapt to raise community awareness on maintaining good health and WASH, as an effort to prevent dengue transmission. By working with partners and communities, these are some collaborative activities to deliver:

  • Health and WASH campaign or dissemination. This includes promoting safe water storage and household water management (see section 2.1.3).
  • Reduce potential mosquito breeding sites. This may be delivered along with larvicide distribution, with dissemination on how to use it properly.

Promote behaviour change through regular community engagement. Health and WASH promotion should go beyond the distribution of information materials. National Society experience indicates that door-to-door engagement and school-level activities can be effective channels for raising awareness.

2.1.3 Diagnosis and Vector Control

Key points
  • Vector control is the only way to interrupt transmission; there is no antiviral treatment.
  • Weekly: cover, empty and clean water containers; remove containers and waste; treat outdoor storage; search and destroy.
  • Strengthen CBS: volunteers detect, report, educate and refer.

Currently, vector control is the only way to interrupt disease transmission. Monitoring and planning control measures for dengue epidemics are crucial for preventing or minimising dengue outbreaks. Currently, there is no specific antiviral treatment for dengue. Early detection and prediction of disease outbreaks are supposed to do for community surveillance systems.[14, 15]

Adapting from WHO guidance[3, 4], there are some preventive actions through environmental sanitation to destroy mosquito breeding sites as follows:

  • Removing discarded containers that serve as mosquito breeding sites
  • Dispose of solid waste properly and remove artiﬁcial habitats that can hold water
  • Cover, empty and clean domestic water storage containers on a weekly basis
  • Apply appropriate insecticides to water storage outdoor containers
  • Search and destroy campaign

To promote this behaviour within the communities, community mobilisation such as community based surveillance (CBS) approach should be strengthened. The community volunteers should be equipped to improve early detection of suspected dengue cases, community surveillance, health education and timely referral for treatment.

2.2 Dengue Preparedness

Key points
  • Know your risks, map them and plan actions (EVCA, Annex 3.1).

To optimise a dengue preparedness, the National Societies along with communities should know what risks they need to mitigate. It makes an effort to prevent the disease transmission becomes outbreak. Risk mapping and identification of actions to reduce the risk are parts of community preparedness. The National Societies could refer to Red Cross and Red Crescent (RCRC) Movement’s Enhanced Vulnerability and Capacity Assessment (EVCA) guidance and toolkits (see Annex 3.1). This EVCA platform has been created to make the guidance, tools and other resources easily accessible to staff and volunteers.

2.2.1 Preparedness on Seasonality and Environmental Patterns

Key points
  • Build a local seasonal risk profile and review it regularly.
  • Regular seasons: start early action about 2 months before the rains. Unpredictable seasons: use triggers (2.3).

Dengue transmission is influenced by environmental and climatic conditions, including seasonal rainfall and temperature patterns. Understanding the seasonal pattern of dengue transmission can help National Societies and their partners prepare resources, strengthen surveillance, and initiate preventive activities before the period of increased transmission.

Preparedness should therefore include the development of a local seasonal dengue risk profile, based on available epidemiological, meteorological, environmental and community-level information. This profile should be reviewed regularly and updated when changes in climate, environment, population movement or dengue transmission patterns are observed.

Figure 2 shows the practical preparedness to understand the local seasonal pattern, monitor environmental and climatic conditions, use seasonal information to prepare before transmission increases, and consider changes in environmental and geographical risk.

2.2.2 Increasing Community Awareness

Key points
  • Give timely, actionable information through several channels; prioritise schools, since children teach families.
  • Go beyond information: support behaviour change and feedback.

Community awareness is an important component of dengue prevention and preparedness. Communities should have access to timely, understandable and locally relevant information that enables them to recognise dengue risks, adopt appropriate preventive practices, identify early symptoms, and know when and where to seek health care.

Awareness activities should not be limited to the dissemination of information. They should aim to support understanding and practical behaviour change, while providing opportunities for communities to raise concerns, identify barriers and provide feedback.

Key points to include in increasing community awareness are as follows:

  • Provide timely and actionable information.
  • Use multiple communication channels, with prioritisation of schools as a community awareness channel. Children are good educator to their family members at their own home and neighbourhood.

2.3 Dengue Early Warning and Early Action[16, 17]

Key points
  • Pre-defined triggers start early action; validate thresholds locally.

2.3.1 Early Warning System

Key points
  • Trigger 1 (climate): rainfall above 100 mm; temperature 15.2 and 15.5°C stages.
  • Trigger 2 (cases): 20+ confirmed per week (about 80 per month).
  • Ladder per district: 0-4 routine, 5-9 early signal, 10-19 alert, 20+ activate, 50+ escalate.

Dengue early warning to conduct early actions should be based on pre-defined threshold or combination of signals. However, there is no universal signals to recommend. Specific thresholds should be defined and validated according to the local epidemiological, climatic and surveillance context. This section explain some examples, adapted from developed early action plan in some countries in the region.

Trigger #1: Average rainfall and temperature

This trigger should consider the signal combination of increased temperature, increased or favourable rainfall, and humidity, if available.

For example,

  • Average rainfall criteria: when average rainfall exceeds 100mm, it creates favourable conditions for dengue outbreaks.

There is typically a lag time of two to four weeks between heavy rainfall and subsequent dengue outbreaks. The Trigger will be reached when climate monitoring by the national hydrological and meteorological department, using an agreed climate monitoring product, indicates temperature conditions associated with an increased risk of dengue transmission.

Temperature criteria:

  • Routine surveillance: when national mean temperature reaches 15.2°C
  • Enhanced Alert: when Temp 15.2–15.5°C
  • Emergency Response: when Temp > 15.5°C or confirmed highland transmission

Trigger # 2: Dengue confirmed cases

The early action trigger will be reached when surveillance data report certain number per week or month. For example, 20 or more laboratory-confirmed dengue cases per week (or the equivalent monthly average of approximately 80 confirmed cases per month).

The trigger thresholds were developed based on the preliminary climate and epidemiological analysis undertaken by the Red Cross Red Crescent Climate Centre using dengue surveillance data (2019–2024). The Trigger#1 is based on climate monitoring by the government bodies of Hydrology and Meteorology using nationally recognised climate monitoring products and, where appropriate, gridded climate datasets. The Trigger#2 is based on the weekly dengue surveillance data that can be accessed through national published epidemiology data (if available) or locally surveillance data monitoring.

2.3.2 Early Action

Key points
  • Intensify community vector control, RCCE and CBS; prioritise high-risk and underserved communities; prepare volunteers; monitor and adapt.

Early action refers to measures implemented when a predefined early-warning trigger indicates an increased risk of dengue transmission. The purpose is to reduce the potential impact of increasing transmission before an outbreak develops or escalates.

Early action should be guided by the locally established trigger and the characteristics of the risk identified. Activities should be coordinated with government health authorities and other relevant partners and should complement existing vector-control and public health measures.

In early action, the activities will be focused on continuing eliminating mosquitoes breeding sites, vector control, and risk communication. Some practical activities could be conducted including:

  • Intensify community based vector control
  • Consider targeted vector control measures
  • Intensify Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE)
  • Strengthen community surveillance and reporting
  • Prioritise high risk and undeserved communities
  • Prepare and mobilise volunteers
  • Monitor the situation and adapt the action.

2.4 Communication & Coordination

Key points
  • Coordinate with authorities and partners; train volunteers; work with local actors.

2.4.1 National Approach and Strategy

Key points
  • Start with Ministry of Health, WHO, humanitarian and academic partners; this strengthens RCCE.

The National Society could start with building communication and coordination with the relevant public authorities (eg. Ministry of Health) and other actors at regional or national level, such as WHO, other humanitarian organisations, academic institutions, etc. This effort also contributes to strengthening Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE).

2.4.2 Sub-national Coordination and Community Mobilisation

Key points
  • Train volunteers (epidemiology, case monitoring, communication); door-to-door visits; accessible IEC materials.
  • Local actors (government, religious leaders, schools, groups) spread messages and bring community information back.

During preparedness and prevention, capacity enhancement to staff and community volunteers is necessary. This contributes to effective community based surveillance (CBS), including a real time surveillance and reporting.

Community volunteers mobilisation

Training or capacity building to community volunteers before mobilise them to the community. The training materials may include dengue epidemiology, case monitoring, and how to communicate with community members.

Household/door-to-door visit. According to National Societies staff and volunteers review, this method is considered effective to raise people awareness.

Distribution of IEC material (dengue epidemiology including symptoms, causes, and preventive action). Community volunteers are capacitated to deliver IEC materials contextually that is easily accessible and understandable by all community members, including the vulnerable or marginalised groups in an appropriate way.

Local actor collaboration

Dengue prevention and response require collaboration with local actors who understand the community, local environment and existing systems. National Societies should establish and maintain coordination with relevant actors at the local level to support timely information sharing, community mobilisation, prevention activities and referral. Collaboration should not be limited to asking local actors to disseminate messages or mobilise communities. Local actors should also be able to bring information from the community back to the National Society and other response partners. The local actors may include local level government, religious leaders, schools, and community groups.

2.5.1 Protection, Gender and Inclusion (PGI) in Dengue Preparedness, Prevention and Early Action

Key points
  • Apply PGI with or without a focal point; prioritise children, elderly, pregnant and lactating women, persons with disabilities, chronic illness, low income.

In assisting communities, National Societies should apply PGI principles within the dengue prevention activities, whether PGI focal point is available or not. National Societies should prioritise vulnerable groups including children, elderly, pregnant and lactating women, persons with disabilities, people living with chronic diseases, and low-income households. This targeted approach ensures that early actions reach those most at risk and maximises the humanitarian impact of limited resources.

A. Equal opportunity to vulnerable or marginalised people. Community consultation, item distribution, and any other community activities should consider access to all members while applying to the local norms.

2.5.2 Community Engagement and Accountability (CEA) in Dengue Prevention, Preparedness and Early Action

Key points
  • Simple local-language IEC with illustrations; mass and social media; complaints and feedback with a standard response time; consult communities; address misconceptions.

CEA is how National Society maintaining accountability toward the communities. Community engagement efforts ensure that in delivering assistance, National Society gives the community opportunity to participate actively. CEA mainstreaming should be done although there is no CEA focal person assigned in place. Below are some good practices in mainstreaming CEA in dengue prevention, preparedness and early action.

Developing information, education and communication (IEC) materials to prevent the disease outbreak. It may include health and hygiene promotion, personal and community protection, etc. IEC can be delivered in many forms, in both electronically or physically. To reach the communities, IEC materials should be contextualised:

  • Using simple terms in local or national language, that can be understood by the community members
  • Appropriate illustration along with the information given, that is easier to help community members who are illiterate

Media engagement to raise awareness of the local population:

  • Mass media, such as radio, television, and newspapers
  • Optimise the utilisation of social media (photo, video, podcast, etc)

Establishing complaint and feedback mechanism. This is appropriate to enhance accountability and improve service quality. Some good practices may include:

  • Community consultation about their preferences to share complaints and feedbacks (e.g. direct communication, message box, hotline call, etc)

Recording and Documenting complaints and feedbacks from the community. It helps the National Society to address any complains and feedbacks collected accordingly. A standard timeline to address problems should be set to improve community engagement and accountability during operations.

Community consultation. It is a vital part to enhance community engagement, that the community members are involved and actively participate to address their needs.

Planning community activities in preparedness and preventing dengue outbreak

Selecting targeted population, including defining the criteria of the targeted people during operations.

Address misconceptions and risk perception

Awareness activities should respond to what communities actually understand, believe and do, rather than assuming that a lack of information is the only barrier to prevention.

2.5.3 Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA) in Dengue Prevention, Preparedness and Early Action

Key points
  • CVA removes financial barriers (repellents, health care, staying home); it is needs-based, not for everyone, and complements vector control.
  • Preparedness: verify data, targeting criteria, markets, providers, registration, coordination and activation procedures.

The cash and voucher assistance (CVA) modality to financially support economically vulnerable people forms a critical part of any response operation and applies to the dengue response. This assistance can mitigate the impact of the disease on households that might otherwise be unable to afford preventive measures or medical treatment. By allocating funds to these groups, financial constraints do not prevent individuals from accessing necessary resources such as mosquito repellents and proper healthcare or even allow them (most vulnerable groups) the economic flexibility to remain at home, reducing the risk of further transmission.[18]

CVA can be used to address financial barriers faced by economically vulnerable households during dengue prevention, preparedness and early action. Where appropriate and feasible, CVA can enable households to access essential goods and services, maintain basic needs during periods of increased dengue risk, and implement recommended preventive measures.

CVA should be considered based on needs, vulnerability, market conditions and the intended preventive or response objective, rather than being automatically provided to all households affected by dengue. Depending on the local context and the results of a needs and market assessment, CVA may support households to:

  • access appropriate mosquito protection and other locally relevant preventive items
  • meet essential household needs when illness or caregiving responsibilities affect household income;
  • access health services where financial barriers are identified
  • support transportation or other necessary costs associated with accessing health services, where appropriate
  • maintain essential household needs during periods when dengue-related illness affects the household's ability to work or generate income

CVA should complement, rather than replace, public health and vector-control interventions.

In the preparedness phase, the National Society should conduct data verification of the target communities. This step is required to ensure the targeted people are the most in need. The real challenges at field are including outdated data of community members. Early identification and verification may help the National Society to deliver CVA in a timely manner and to reach out the most in need.

During preparedness, the National Society should assess whether CVA is an appropriate modality for the anticipated dengue risk and establish the systems required for timely delivery.

This should include:

  • identifying potentially vulnerable and at-risk communities
  • reviewing and verifying available population and vulnerability data
  • identifying appropriate targeting criteria
  • assessing market availability and functionality
  • identifying potential financial service providers and delivery mechanisms
  • establishing or reviewing beneficiary registration and verification processes
  • agreeing on coordination arrangements with relevant authorities and partners
  • preparing the operational procedures required for rapid activation of CVA.

Chapter 3. Dengue Outbreak Response

3.1 Case Management

Key points
  • No specific treatment; early detection, warning signs and case management keep fatality below 1%.

Dengue is mostly found in tropical and sub-tropical climates worldwide, mostly in urban and semi-urban areas.[1] However recent cases show the increased prevalence in rural areas across some countries in Asia Pacific. There is no specific treatment for dengue, however, the timely detection of cases, identifying any warning signs of severe dengue infection, and appropriate case management are key actions to lower case fatality rates to less than 1%.[5]

3.1.1 Symptoms and Cases Monitoring

Key points
  • Most infections have no symptoms; a second infection raises the risk of severe dengue.
  • Mild: keep monitoring. Moderate to severe, or getting worse: hospitalise.
  • Document cases (template below) and check blood transfusion capacity.

Most people who get dengue do not show symptoms. Individuals who are infected for the second time are at greater risk of severe dengue. For those who do, the most common symptoms[1, 4] to observe are:

3.1.2 Community Mobilisation

Key points
  • Mobilise volunteers to hospitals; supply LLINs, medicine and test kits; protect staff and volunteers with repellent and nets.

National Society mobilise volunteers to hospitals and provided long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), medicine, and testing kits to health facilities.

Adequate health safety measures to the staff and community volunteers should be taken, such as using of mosquito repellent and mosquito nets.

3.1.3 Transition to Recovery

Key points
  • Plan the move to recovery with authorities: review cases, surveillance, needs, local capacity, volunteers, feedback and handover.

The transition from outbreak response to recovery should begin when the immediate public health emergency has stabilised and the intensity of response activities can gradually be reduced. The transition should be planned and coordinated, rather than treated as the end of the response operation.

The National Society, in coordination with relevant authorities and partners, should assess whether the situation has stabilised and determine which response activities can be reduced, maintained or integrated into routine programmes and services.

Before scaling down response activities, the National Society should review:

  • the epidemiological situation and trends in dengue cases
  • the status of ongoing surveillance and early-warning mechanisms
  • community needs and remaining vulnerabilities
  • the capacity of local health and community systems to continue relevant activities
  • the status of volunteer mobilisation and support
  • outstanding community feedback and concerns
  • resources and activities that need to be transferred to routine programmes or other responsible actors

The criteria for transition should be agreed with relevant health authorities and other partners and should take into account the local context and national outbreak management protocols.

3.2 Cross-cutting Theme in Dengue Outbreak Response

3.2.1 Protection, Gender and Inclusion (PGI) in Dengue Outbreak Response

Key points
  • Outbreaks widen inequalities: ask who is missing, find barriers, adapt, listen, monitor (cards below).

During a dengue outbreak, existing inequalities and barriers to accessing health information and services may become more pronounced. The National Society should therefore integrate PGI considerations throughout the outbreak response to ensure that affected people can access information, assistance and health services safely, equitably and with dignity.

During the initial and ongoing operation, the National Society should identify population groups that may experience different levels of exposure, vulnerability or access to dengue-related services. This should not assume that all members of a particular group have the same needs. The response should use available data and community feedback to understand which barriers exist and for whom.

3.2.2 Community Engagement and Accountability (CEA) in Dengue Outbreak Response

Key points
  • Before closing, collect, document, act on and report back community feedback.

Before concluding the response, the National Society should provide opportunities for affected communities to provide feedback on the response and identify remaining needs or concerns. Feedback should be:

  • collected through appropriate community channels
  • documented and analysed
  • addressed where action is possible
  • communicated back to communities.

3.2.3 Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA) in Dengue Outbreak Response

Key points
  • Consider CVA when illness creates financial barriers; assess needs, costs, markets and other assistance; complement the health response.

CVA may be considered as part of the dengue outbreak response when affected households experience financial barriers that limit their ability to meet essential needs, access health services or cope with the consequences of dengue-related illness.

CVA should be based on a needs assessment and appropriate targeting, taking into account the socioeconomic impact of the outbreak, household vulnerability, market conditions and the availability of other forms of assistance. It should complement public health, health-care and community-based interventions rather than replace them.

Following the identification of an outbreak, the National Society should assess whether affected households are experiencing financial barriers that require CVA. The assessment may consider:

  • loss or reduction of household income due to illness or caregiving responsibilities;
  • costs associated with accessing health services;
  • transportation or other essential costs related to seeking care;
  • ability to meet essential household needs during illness;
  • access to appropriate dengue prevention measures;
  • existing social protection or other assistance available to households; and
  • market availability and functionality.

3.3 Planning, Monitoring, Evaluation and Reporting (PMER) in Dengue Outbreak Response

3.3.1 Evaluation, Reporting and Learning

Key points
  • Structured review of what worked; share results with communities; feed lessons into plans and EAPs; learn across countries.

The transition period should include a structured review of what worked, what did not work, and what should be changed before the next dengue season or outbreak. Lessons learned should be shared with relevant stakeholders and incorporated into preparedness plans, early-action protocols and other relevant tools. The data collection specifically recommends sharing monitoring and evaluation results with communities, documenting lessons after a completed response, and promoting cross-country learning within the region

Chapter 4. RCRC Movement Support

4.1 Technical Support

Key points
  • Ask the IFRC country delegation, cluster or regional office early for analysis, planning, early-action design, CBS/RCCE, vector control, PGI/CEA, MEL and resources.

National Societies can engage with the IFRC country delegation, cluster or regional structures, and other relevant RCRC Movement partners to access technical support according to their needs and capacities.

Technical support may include assistance to analyse the dengue situation, assess risks and needs, develop or review preparedness and response plans, establish early-warning and early-action mechanisms, and strengthen monitoring and learning.

Depending on the context, National Societies may seek technical assistance in areas such as:

  • Dengue risk and epidemiological analysis, including interpretation of available surveillance and environmental information.
  • Preparedness and response planning, including development or review of dengue response plans and operational priorities.
  • Early warning and early action, including identification of appropriate indicators, triggers and actions.
  • Community-based surveillance and risk communication, including RCCE approaches.
  • Vector control and community-level prevention, in coordination with relevant health authorities.
  • PGI and CEA, to ensure that activities are inclusive, accessible and responsive to community needs.
  • MEL, including development of monitoring frameworks, indicators, documentation and learning mechanisms.
  • Operational planning and resource mobilisation, where additional support is required.
  • National Societies should communicate their technical support needs as early as possible, particularly when dengue risk is increasing or an early-warning trigger has been reached. Early engagement can help ensure that technical advice is available before the situation escalates.

4.2 IFRC Disaster Response Emergency Fund (DREF)

Key points
  • DREF is a fast, localised fund for dengue training, awareness, materials, CBS and prevention messaging.

Dengue response is aligned with the IFRC's regional epidemic preparedness and control priorities, focusing on supporting the Host National Society through training, awareness campaigns, distribution of educational materials, community-based surveillance, and communication of key dengue prevention messages in collaboration with the local authorities and stakeholders. Therefore, National Societies could access DREF to support dengue operations.

The IFRC DREF is an efficient, fast, transparent, and localised way of getting funding directly to local humanitarian actors – both before and after crisis hits.[20] On the left side is a summary of DREF application process the National Society should follow.[21]

4.3 RCRC Movement Collaboration

Key points
  • Host and Participating National Societies can share flexible funds and cross-border learning.

As a Movement, the Host National Society (HNS) and Participating National Societies (PNS) could collaborate in responding to hazards including dengue. One of support can be accessed is contingency or flexible fund from PNSs to the National Society. The mechanism and qualification are varied, based on each other agreement. The form and scope of collaboration should be agreed between the relevant National Societies and aligned with the needs, capacities and priorities of the HNS.

Besides funding support, National Societies within the Movement could collaborate to respond in dengue. Since dengue is highly occurring in the region of Asia Pacific, coordination between neighbouring National Societies may improve each other efforts in preventing to respond dengue, particularly in cross border areas.

Dengue transmission and population movement do not necessarily follow administrative or national borders. In areas where communities, transport routes or population movements connect neighbouring countries, collaboration between National Societies can strengthen preparedness and response.

National Societies within the region can use existing Movement networks to share experiences before, during and after dengue operations. This can help National Societies learn from different approaches to prevention, early action, outbreak response, PGI, CEA, volunteer mobilisation and MEL.
